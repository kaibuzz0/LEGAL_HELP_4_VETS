Military Immigration: Citizenship, Family, Deportation Defense

FOREIGN CITIZENSHIP & MILITARY IMMIGRATION

Military service creates special immigration protections and pathways for servicemembers, veterans, and their families. This section explains parole in place, military naturalization, family petitions, and deportation defense.

What This Is and Who Qualifies

Military service can create faster or safer pathways to U.S. citizenship and can protect non-citizen family members from immigration problems. This page covers military naturalization, citizenship for surviving family members, family immigration, parole in place, deportation defense, and the special rules that apply to veterans with bad paper. [67] [66] [73]

Who qualifies: Non-citizen servicemembers and veterans with honorable service, current and former family members of U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident servicemembers, and veterans facing removal because of a criminal or discharge issue. Eligibility depends on service status, discharge characterization, and immigration history. [67] [77]

Military Naturalization (Expedited Citizenship)

Under sections 328 and 329 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), eligible servicemembers and veterans can naturalize without the usual five-year residence requirement or even while serving abroad. [77] [67]

  1. Check eligibility: Generally, you must have served honorably during wartime or peacetime, be a lawful permanent resident (with some exceptions), and meet character requirements. [77]
  2. Complete N-400 (Application for Naturalization). [68]
  3. Request military certification: Submit USCIS Form N-426 (Request for Certification of Military or Naval Service) with your service record. [69]
  4. File online or by mail with USCIS, and request expedited processing based on military service. [67]
  5. Attend biometrics and interview. Some applicants may be eligible for overseas processing or expedited oath ceremonies. [67]

Surviving spouses and children: Certain family members of servicemembers who died during qualifying service may apply for naturalization under INA section 319(d). [77]

Family Immigration for Servicemembers and Veterans

U.S. citizen and lawful permanent resident servicemembers can petition for spouses, children, parents, and siblings. Veterans can also petition family members if they are citizens or permanent residents. [70] [71]

  1. File Form I-130 (Petition for Alien Relative) for each qualifying family member. [70]
  2. If the family member is already in the U.S. and eligible, file Form I-485 (Application to Register Permanent Residence or Adjust Status) to adjust status. [71]
  3. If the family member is outside the U.S., the case goes to the National Visa Center and then a U.S. consulate for immigrant visa processing. [70]
  4. Evidence to attach: birth and marriage certificates, divorce decrees, proof of petitioner’s citizenship or LPR status, military orders or DD-214, and affidavit of support if required.
  5. Expedite requests: Military deployment or urgent circumstances may support an expedite request with USCIS. [67]

Parole in Place: Protect Spouses, Parents, and Children

Parole in place allows certain undocumented family members of U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident servicemembers and veterans to remain in the U.S. and apply for adjustment of status without having to leave the country first. [66]

  1. Who qualifies: Spouses, parents, and unmarried minor children (including stepchildren) of active-duty, Selected Reserve, or former servicemembers. [66]
  2. How to request: Submit Form I-131 (Application for Travel Document) with a cover letter explaining the military connection and requesting parole in place. [66]
  3. Attach: proof of family relationship, proof of the servicemember’s status (DD-214, military ID, orders), and evidence of favorable discretionary factors.
  4. After parole is granted, the family member may be eligible to apply for adjustment of status if a visa is immediately available. [66]

If a Veteran Faces Deportation or Removal

Non-citizen veterans with a less-than-honorable discharge or a criminal conviction can face deportation. There are specialized programs and defenses. [73] [29]

  1. Contact ICE veterans resources: ICE maintains a Military Veterans and Servicemembers email box to help locate veterans in removal proceedings and coordinate with VA. [73]
  2. Request a discharge upgrade: If the bad discharge is connected to PTSD, TBI, MST, or other service conditions, file DD Form 149 or 293. A better discharge characterization can help immigration and VA cases. [29] [39]
  3. Apply for VA benefits anyway: File a VA claim to trigger a Character of Discharge review. [38]
  4. Find an immigration attorney: Look for one who understands military service, post-conviction relief, and veterans treatment courts. [13]
  5. Post-conviction relief: In some states, a criminal conviction can be reopened if the veteran was not advised that a plea would lead to deportation. This requires a state-court criminal attorney.

VAWA Self-Petition for Abused Family Members

Spouses, children, and parents of U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents who have been abused may self-petition for immigration status without the abuser’s cooperation. Military families are not exempt from this protection. [72]

  1. File Form I-360 (Petition for Amerasian, Widow(er), or Special Immigrant) with the VAWA self-petition evidence. [72]
  2. Attach: proof of relationship, proof of abuser’s status, evidence of abuse (police reports, medical records, affidavits), and evidence of good moral character.
  3. Seek help safely: The National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 can help with safety planning. [72]

Document Checklist for Military Immigration Cases

  • DD-214 and any discharge upgrade decisions
  • Military orders, ID cards, or proof of current service
  • Birth, marriage, divorce, and death certificates
  • USCIS forms N-400, N-426, I-130, I-485, I-131, or I-360 as applicable
  • Proof of petitioner’s U.S. citizenship or LPR status
  • Criminal records and court dispositions (if any)
  • Medical and mental health records showing PTSD/TBI/MST
  • Copy of every filing and receipt notice from USCIS or ICE

What If They Say No

  • Naturalization denied: You may reapply or appeal. If the issue is a criminal record or discharge characterization, fix those first. [67]
  • Family petition denied: Check whether the denial is because of missing documents, inadmissibility, or priority date. A waiver may be available. [70]
  • Parole in place denied: Reapply with stronger evidence of military connection and favorable discretion. [66]
  • In removal proceedings: Request continuances to pursue discharge upgrades and VA benefits; coordinate with ICE veterans resources and an immigration attorney. [73]

Who Qualifies for Military Immigration Relief

  • Active-duty servicemembers and certain veterans
  • Spouses, children, and parents of servicemembers (for parole in place)
  • Family members eligible for adjustment of status through a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident relative
  • Surviving spouses and children of servicemembers who died in service (for certain survivor benefits)

Parole in Place (PIP) for Military Families

Parole in place allows certain undocumented spouses, children, and parents of U.S. military members to remain in the U.S. while seeking adjustment of status. [66]

  1. Confirm the servicemember is on active duty or is a veteran.
  2. Gather proof of family relationship and the servicemember's military status: birth/marriage certificate, DD-214 or current orders, military ID.
  3. Prepare a cover letter explaining the military connection and why PIP is warranted.
  4. File the PIP request with the local USCIS office. Procedures vary by district; check USCIS guidance or consult an immigration attorney.
Sample cover letter: "I am the [spouse/child/parent] of [servicemember name], who is currently serving / has served in the [branch]. I am requesting parole in place under existing military family relief policies. Enclosed are proof of relationship and proof of military service. Granting parole will protect military readiness and family unity."
Military Naturalization (INA 328 and 329)

Certain servicemembers and veterans may naturalize under special provisions. [67] [77]

  • INA 328: At least one year of honorable peacetime service; no continuous residence requirement while serving.
  • INA 329: Any period of service during designated hostilities; may naturalize without prior residence.
  • Posthumous naturalization: Available for servicemembers who died during qualifying service.
  1. Confirm eligibility and that you are a lawful permanent resident (with limited exceptions).
  2. Complete USCIS Form N-400 (Application for Naturalization). [68]
  3. Request Form N-426 (Request for Certification of Military or Naval Service) through your service branch. [69]
  4. Submit fingerprints, photos, and required fees or fee waiver.
  5. Attend interview and oath ceremony.
Spouse / Children Adjustment of Status

U.S. citizen servicemembers and veterans can petition spouses, children, and parents for lawful permanent residence.

  1. File Form I-130 (Petition for Alien Relative) with USCIS. [70]
  2. When the priority date is current (or immediately if the petitioner is a U.S. citizen and the beneficiary is an immediate relative), file Form I-485 (Application to Register Permanent Residence or Adjust Status). [71]
  3. Submit supporting documents: birth/marriage certificates, proof of petitioner's citizenship, financial sponsorship (I-864), medical exam, and any PIP grant.
  4. Attend biometrics and interview.
Deportation Defense for Veterans

Veterans facing removal may have unique defenses and forms of relief. [73]

  • Contact an immigration attorney or accredited representative immediately.
  • Gather proof of military service, VA benefits, treatment records, and family ties in the U.S.
  • Request a Character of Discharge review from VA if discharge is a barrier to benefits.
  • Explore cancellation of removal, adjustment of status, asylum, or other relief based on individual facts.
  • Ask your attorney whether military service can be raised as a favorable discretionary factor.
VAWA Self-Petition Route

If a noncitizen spouse, child, or parent of a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident servicemember has been abused, they may self-petition under the Violence Against Women Act. [72]

  1. Consult an attorney or VAWA-experienced advocate. This is sensitive and evidence-intensive.
  2. Gather evidence of the qualifying relationship, abuse, good moral character, and residence.
  3. File the self-petition with USCIS. The abuser is not notified.
Document Checklist
  • DD-214 or current military orders
  • Birth, marriage, and divorce certificates
  • Proof of petitioner U.S. citizenship (passport, birth certificate, naturalization certificate)
  • I-94, passport, and prior immigration documents
  • VA records, treatment records, character references
  • Financial documents for sponsorship (I-864)
  • Passport-style photos and medical exam (I-693) for adjustment applicants
Records to Keep
  • Copies of every USCIS form and filing fee receipt
  • USCIS receipt notices and biometrics appointments
  • Attorney correspondence and retainer agreements
  • Call log with USCIS, ICE, and court officials
  • Certified translations of foreign-language documents
YOUR NEXT ACTION: If you are a servicemember or veteran with immigration concerns, consult an immigration attorney or accredited representative. Do not navigate removal proceedings or complex adjustment cases alone.
